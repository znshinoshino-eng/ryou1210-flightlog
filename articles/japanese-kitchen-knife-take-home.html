Taking a Japanese Kitchen Knife Home: Trains, the Airport and the Tax-Free Check

Travel prep · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: a kitchen knife is one of the best souvenirs you can take home from Japan, and getting it there is easy if you do three things. Keep it properly packed (intact shop packaging counts) while you ride trains. Put it in your checked baggage, not your carry-on. And if you bought it tax-free, do the customs check at the airport before you drop your suitcase, because you can't get a checked bag back for it.

Below is what the rules actually say, step by step, from the shop to your flight home. I've quoted the official wording where it matters, because a few of these rules are more conditional than people think.

Walking around with a new knife: what Japanese law says

Japan's Firearms and Swords Control Law (Article 22) covers blades longer than 6 cm. The Tokyo Metropolitan Police Department's summary is that nobody may carry one "except for work or another legitimate reason", and the penalty is up to 2 years' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥300,000.

The good news for shoppers: the same police page gives, as an example of a legitimate reason, "buying a knife at a shop and taking it home". Kagoshima Prefectural Police list the same example. My practical advice: keep it wrapped, go fairly directly from the shop to your hotel, and treat it as luggage rather than something you carry around with you for the rest of the day.

Taking it on trains and the shinkansen

Since 1 April 2019, JR East and JR Central have treated knives as items you can't take on board unless they are packed. JR Central's English page lists "sharp objects" as prohibited, with a footnote: "Sharp objects packed as to not hurt other customers are allowed." JR East's wording (in Japanese) is the same idea: knives are excluded from the ban if they are packed so that they pose no risk of harm to other passengers. JR East's illustration lists "exposed blades" among the banned items, and JR Central's own list of examples names "Knives, Kitchen Knives etc." and "Improperly packaged blades".

Both railways point to a packing guideline published by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) in December 2018. For blades over 6 cm, it gives two example methods:

  • Keep the shop packaging intact. The guideline counts it as packed if "the packaging from purchase at a retail shop is still intact".
  • Or pack it yourself: put the blade in a sheath or case (plastic, leather, etc.), or cover the edge with cardboard, wrap the whole knife in newspaper or similar, and then put it in a sturdy bag, box or case so it can't poke out.

In practice, that means: don't unbox it in your hotel room and then ride the shinkansen with it loose in your backpack. If you've already opened it, rewrap it as above before you travel. If you'd rather not carry it at all, you can send your suitcase ahead with luggage forwarding; check that service's own rules on knives first.

At the airport: checked baggage only

The airport and airline sources I checked all tell you to keep knives out of the cabin, though Narita's wording is that you "basically" cannot carry them on board.

MLIT's aviation page adds that some airlines may have stricter rules, so "follow each airline's instructions". If you fly a different airline, check its restricted-items page before you pack.

One more thing worth knowing: Narita says security staff may open checked bags without the owner present to take out dangerous goods, so pack the knife so it's obviously a wrapped kitchen knife and won't cut anyone who opens the case.

No Japanese source I found sets a specific packing rule for knives in checked bags. If you're flying to the US, though, the TSA says "Any sharp objects in checked bags should be sheathed or securely wrapped to prevent injury to baggage handlers and inspectors." The UK government lists knives as "No" for hand luggage and "Yes (check with your airline)" for hold luggage. The train-packing method above (sheath or cardboard, then wrapped, then in a box) works well for this too.

Bought it tax-free? Do the customs check before bag drop

This is the step to plan for. Since 1 November 2026, Japan's tax-free shopping works as a refund: you pay the tax at the shop and the refund is generally processed after Customs confirms at departure that the goods are leaving Japan. (Full details in our tax-free refund guide.)

The Japan Tourism Agency is clear about the order: "You must complete the customs inspection before checking in your baggage." and "Checked baggage cannot be retrieved once it has been checked in." It also asks you to "ensure that all purchased goods can be presented to customs" when you leave Japan. In Japanese it adds that if you don't have even one of the items, none of the items, including the others, can be confirmed or refunded, and that customs decides per purchase record.

Put that together with "knives go in checked baggage" and you get this order on departure day:

  1. Arrive with the knife packed in your suitcase or a bag you can open easily, with your passport.
  2. Use the tax-free procedure terminal before check-in. At Haneda, these are at Terminals 2 and 3, before the 3rd floor security check (Haneda says Terminal 1 has no tax refund service).
  3. If the result is green, the customs procedure is finished. If it's red, take the goods to the customs inspection area and show them.
  4. Then make sure the knife is in the suitcase you're checking, and drop the bag.

The customs check has to happen within 90 days of purchase. Osaka Customs' leaflet for Kansai Airport says the same thing as the Japan Tourism Agency: "Please complete the tax refund procedure before checking in your baggage." No official source I found covers knives specifically under the tax-free check, so the safe assumption is to treat it like any other purchase and keep it reachable until customs is done.

Shipping it home by Japan Post instead

If you'd rather not fly with it, Japan Post accepts kitchen knives in international mail, but only if they're packed to its standard. Its rules for "knives etc." (which it says include kitchen knives, scissors and cutter knives) are:

  • Always protect the blade with sturdy material: a sheath or case, or if there isn't one, a strong wooden or plastic cover.
  • Wrap the whole item in cushioning so it can't move.
  • Put it in a strong, rigid box and fill the gaps with cushioning so it can't move and pierce the box.

Japan Post warns that the shop's packaging alone may be pierced in handling, so you need to wrap the whole knife in thick cushioning, and it says it will refuse to accept items that don't meet these conditions. Your destination country may also have its own import rules, so check those before you post it.
