Wheelchair and Step-Free Travel on Japanese Trains: How to Get Assistance

Accessible travel guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked October 2026

Short answer: Tell the station staff that you use a wheelchair. Rail operators say staff can take you to the platform and help you on and off the train. Give yourself extra time. For the shinkansen, book a wheelchair seat or space ahead. On JR East, check with staff before you buy your ticket, because some types of wheelchair can't board some trains.

I work on Japan's railways. This guide sticks to what the operators themselves say, so you know what to expect and where to ask.

Getting help at the station: ask the staff

On ordinary trains, the first step is to find a member of station staff (the ticket gate is the easiest place to look) and tell them where you're going.

  • JR East (Tokyo and eastern Japan) says on its English accessibility page that "passengers using wheelchairs are requested to approach a station staff member," and that "the staff member will escort you to the platform and help you get on or off the train."
  • Toei Subway (one of Tokyo's two subway operators) has portable ramps so wheelchair users can board or leave trains "at any point of the platform." It asks you to "contact the nearest station staff member if you wish to use one."
  • JNTO, Japan's national tourism organization, gives the same advice: if you use a wheelchair, "you can ask the station staff for assistance getting on and off the train." Its guide also suggests telling staff where you'll get off, so someone can be there to meet you.

Two things to plan for. First, it takes time. JR East says "it may take some time to escort you to the platform, so please come to the station a little early." No operator I checked gives a set number of minutes. Second, JR East's Japanese-language page asks wheelchair users to contact them in advance where possible. It's phrased as a request for your cooperation, not a rule.

If you'd like to ask something in English before you travel, the JR EAST Infoline answers on (050) 2016-1603, or +81-50-2016-1603 from abroad. It's open 10:00 to 18:00 every day except during the year-end and New Year holidays. It covers station facilities and similar questions, but it is "an information service and does not accept reservations."

If the city's networks are new to you, see how Tokyo's JR, Tokyo Metro and Toei lines fit together. Each company runs its own stations.

Check your wheelchair before you buy a ticket

This is the part that catches people out. JR East says on its English page: "If you use a wheelchair, you may not be able to board the train depending on the type of wheelchair you use … So be sure to check with us before you buy your ticket." Its FAQ adds that boarding "may not be possible depending on the type of wheelchair, the train you plan to use, and the station."

The size limits JR East states are:

ItemJR East limit
Length and heightNo more than 120 cm
WidthNo more than 70 cm
Bigger than this?JR East's Japanese page asks you to consult them

Mobility scooters (power wheelchairs steered with handlebars) have extra conditions. JR East says they can use some trains if they meet size, right-angle-turn and 180-degree-turn requirements. JR Central's English guide gives the details: within 70 cm wide and 120 cm long and high; able to "turn a 0.9m-wide, right angle within five adjustments" and "a 1.0m-wide, right angle without making any adjustments"; able to "turn 180 degrees within a space that's less than 1.8m wide". Inside stations, the same guide asks you to drive slowly and "do not exceed 2km/h". The Japan Rail Pass site sums it up: scooter-type power wheelchairs "are not generally permitted … although they may be used when certain conditions are satisfied."

Shinkansen: booking a wheelchair seat or space

On the bullet train you'll want a reserved spot. On the Tokaido Shinkansen (Tokyo–Nagoya–Kyoto–Shin-Osaka), JR Central says every train has wheelchair seats, and some trains run with N700S cars that have six wheelchair spaces.

The official booking site smartEX (for the Tokaido, Sanyo and Kyushu shinkansen) lists wheelchair seats on Nozomi, Hikari, Kodama, Mizuho, Sakura and Tsubame trains. On a 16-car Tokaido–Sanyo train they're in Car 11. There are three types:

smartEX seat typeWhat it is
Wheelchair accessible seat (B)You can stay in your wheelchair or move to a train seat
Wheelchair space (E)You stay in your wheelchair
Companion seat (A)For the person travelling with and helping you

On smartEX you can book "from 10:00 am one month prior to boarding date, up to 4 minutes before the departure time." You can't book more than one type at once, so book your seat and your companion's seat separately.

You can also book through JR Central by phone or at a station. Station and phone bookings open at 10:00 one month before your travel date. JR Central's English page says wheelchair seats need a reservation and it accepts them "until two days prior to your boarding." Its web request form is in Japanese and also closes two days before travel. Your preferred train isn't guaranteed. The station phone lines below are open 7:00 to 19:00. The automated menu is in Japanese; JR Central's page gives a phrase for asking the staff member for English.

JR East shinkansen (to Tohoku, Niigata, Hokuriku and so on) also has a Japanese-language web request form. It accepts requests from one month to two days before travel, and it doesn't guarantee your train. You can't use it if your trip involves a connection to another shinkansen or limited express, if you use a scooter-type power wheelchair, if you want the multi-purpose room, or if you already hold a ticket.

With a Japan Rail Pass: the official JR Pass site says wheelchair seats are available "on most of the Shinkansen and limited express trains on the conventional lines." You book them at a JR ticket office (Midori-no-madoguchi) "from 10:00 am daily, for up to one month in advance." It also warns that if the seats are full, "you will not be allowed to board the train that you want to get on", so book early. See which rail passes are worth it, and how to ride the shinkansen for platforms and car numbers.

Tokaido Shinkansen trains also have a multi-purpose room, which JR Central says "is primarily for disabled passengers." Ask about it when you book.

Finding elevators and step-free routes

  • Toei Subway says it has installed elevators at all of its stations "to provide at least one barrier-free route between ground level, ticket gate level and platform level." It is also still upgrading elevators so each one can take a wheelchair.
  • Tokyo Metro has an English station search where you can filter by facility: a step-free "one route", elevators inside or outside the gate, stairlifts, and wheelchair-accessible toilets. Each station has its own accessibility page, which is worth a look before you go.
  • Nationwide, JNTO recommends Ecomo-rakuraku (ecomo-rakuraku.jp/en), which shows how accessible stations are across Japan.

If you can't find the step-free route, ask station staff.

Accessible toilets

JR East says it has "updated and redesigned many of our restrooms to provide accessibility for wheelchair passengers." In Japanese they're called バリアフリートイレ (barrier-free toilet) or 多機能トイレ (multi-function toilet). Tokyo Metro's station search shows whether a wheelchair-accessible toilet is inside or outside the ticket gate.

Disability fare discounts

Japanese railways offer disability discounts, but going by JR West's wording they're tied to disability certificates issued in Japan. JR West says the 50% discount needs a certificate "issued by each local government" (各自治体で発行する) with a photo and a Class 1 or Class 2 entry in the railway fare field. Travelling alone, it applies only on one-way trips over 100 km. Travelling with a carer, Class 1 holders get it at any distance. Tokyo Metro also has a 50% discount, but with conditions: travelling alone, it covers regular tickets only for trips of 101 km or more (not IC cards), and you may be asked to show your certificate. I couldn't find an official statement about certificates issued outside Japan, so ask at the ticket office rather than counting on a discount.

A quick checklist

  • Check your wheelchair's size against JR East's 120 cm × 70 cm limits. If you use a scooter, check the extra conditions.
  • Book shinkansen wheelchair seats as early as you can. Booking opens at 10:00 one month before travel.
  • At each station, tell the staff at the gate where you're going, and arrive early.
  • Look up elevators and accessible toilets on the operator's station pages before the day.
  • Heavy suitcases? Send them ahead with luggage forwarding. For door-to-door trips, a taxi or a private car transfer can be easier than a train.
