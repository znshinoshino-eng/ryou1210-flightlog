Sendai and Matsushima from Tokyo by Shinkansen

Sightseeing guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: yes, you can do Sendai and Matsushima in a day from Tokyo — the Hayabusa takes 90 minutes each way, and Matsushima is 40 minutes further by local train. But the day is long, and Sendai itself deserves an afternoon, so if you can spare one night, stay over and let the trip breathe. Either way, this is one of the easiest first tastes of Tohoku: no car, no complicated connections, just two trains and a boat.

Matsushima is one of Japan's Three Views, a bay scattered with about 260 pine-covered islands. Sendai, the city you pass through to get there, is Tohoku's biggest city and the home of Date Masamune, the one-eyed lord whose castle and mausoleum still anchor the place.

Tokyo to Sendai: the Hayabusa is the whole trick

The fastest train is the Hayabusa on the Tohoku Shinkansen. It covers Tokyo to Sendai in 1 hour 30 to 1 hour 38 minutes, and a reserved seat costs ¥11,630 one way at the time of writing (regular season, paper ticket). Book the same seat as an e-ticket on JR-EAST Train Reservation and it is ¥11,430. Every seat on the Hayabusa is reserved, so you need a seat booking before you board — there is no standing in an unreserved car as a fallback.

The slower Yamabiko takes 1 hour 46 minutes to 2 hours 24 minutes and does have unreserved cars, which makes it the backup if the Hayabusa you want is full. Trains run two or three times an hour for most of the day, and seats go on sale at 10:00 Japan time, one month before travel. A comfortable day-trip departure is Hayabusa 5, which leaves Tokyo at 08:18 and reaches Sendai at 09:48 — the first trains leave from 6:32 if you want the earliest possible start.

Book on JR-EAST Train Reservation if you are buying from abroad, or at a station machine or ticket office once you are in Japan. Our shinkansen ticket guide walks through the options.

Sendai to Matsushima: take the Senseki Line, not the Tohoku Line

From Sendai Station, take the JR Senseki Line to Matsushimakaigan Station: about 40 minutes and ¥440 one way at the time of writing. The Senseki Line platforms (9 and 10) are underground, on a different level from the shinkansen, so allow the 10 minutes JR East lists as the standard transfer time — more if you have heavy luggage. Take a train bound for Ishinomaki or Takagimachi, and check the departure board: some trains turn back early and never reach the coast.

The trap is the station name. Matsushima Station, on the JR Tohoku Line, sounds like the same place and is not: it is about a 25-minute walk from the waterfront. You want Matsushimakaigan — literally "Matsushima coast" — which puts the cruise pier, Zuiganji and Godaido all within a short walk. If you are staying overnight, leave big bags in a locker at Sendai Station and take only a day pack to the bay; see our coin locker guide for sizes and prices.

What to do in Matsushima

Plan on four to five hours at the bay. Everything is on foot from Matsushimakaigan Station, and this order works well:

  • The bay cruise first. The Nioumaru boat loops around the islands in about 50 minutes, leaving hourly from 09:00 to 16:00 (last sailing 15:00 in winter). An adult ticket is ¥1,500, children 6–12 pay ¥750, and the open upper deck costs an extra ¥600 for adults and ¥300 for children, paid on board. No reservation is needed — buy at the ticket window by the pier, cash or PayPay — but the boat is first come, first served for seats, so arrive a little early. In winter, strong wind can cancel sailings, so check that the boats are running before you leave Sendai.
  • Godaido Hall. The small temple hall on its own island right by the pier, reached by short bridges. It is free, and it is the classic Matsushima photograph.
  • Fukuurajima Island. Cross the 252-metre vermilion Fukuurabashi bridge — the "encounter bridge" — to a quiet botanical island with walking trails and viewpoints back across the bay. Crossing costs ¥300 for adults and ¥100 for school-age children at the time of writing, and the bridge is open 08:30–17:00 from April to October and until 16:30 from November to March. English guides that still say ¥200 are out of date.

Lunch is easy around the pier: oysters and seafood bowls are the local thing, and the fish market near the station is good for a quick bowl if the restaurants have queues.

An afternoon in Sendai

If you stay the night — or get back from Matsushima by mid-afternoon — Sendai rewards a few unhurried hours. The Loople Sendai sightseeing loop bus is the practical way around: a 15-stop, roughly 70-minute circuit from the West Exit bus pool (stop 16) that links the castle ruins, Zuihoden and Osaki Hachimangu, the hilltop sights the subway does not reach. A single ride is ¥260 and a day pass ¥630, so the pass pays for itself on the third ride. Buses run about every 20 minutes on weekdays and every 15 minutes on weekends, with the first departure at 09:00 and the last at 16:00. Pay with an IC card, because cash on board means coins and ¥1,000 notes only.

Zuihoden, Date Masamune's mausoleum, is the stop to get off for: an ornate black-and-gold hall in a cedar forest, built in 1637 and rebuilt in 1979. Admission is ¥570 for adults. The Sendai Castle ruins sit on a hill above the city, with views back over Sendai and a statue of Masamune on horseback. For dinner, Sendai's signature is gyutan — grilled beef tongue — and the station building is full of long-running gyutan restaurants, so you do not need to hunt for one.

Day trip or one night? The money

A day trip works if you leave Tokyo on the 08:18 Hayabusa: you are on the water by 11:00, done with the temples by mid-afternoon, and back in Sendai with time for a Loople loop and dinner before an evening train home. Hayabusa trains run well into the evening, but check the last departure for your date on JR-EAST Train Reservation before you settle in for dinner.

Staying one night costs little extra and fixes the two pressure points: Zuiganji's early winter closing and the Loople's 16:00 last bus. Sleep in Sendai, do the city properly in the morning, and take a late-morning train to Matsushima — or reverse it and spend the night in Matsushima itself, where the bay is quiet after the day-trippers leave.

Two passes are worth checking against that total. The Sendai Area Pass costs ¥1,420 for one day and covers the Senseki Line to Matsushima, the Loople bus, the subway and city buses — it beats paying separately, since the Matsushima return (¥880) plus a Loople day pass (¥630) already comes to ¥1,510. You need a non-Japanese passport to buy it, at the JR East Travel Service Center on the 3rd floor of Sendai Station or a reserved-seat machine with a passport reader. The JR EAST PASS is the bigger one: 5 days for ¥35,000, covering all JR East lines including shinkansen reserved seats. A Tokyo–Sendai return alone (¥23,260) does not pay for it — but if your trip also goes to Yamadera, Aomori or Nikko, it starts to win. A few things railway staff wish visitors knew

  • Book the Hayabusa, don't just turn up. All seats are reserved, and the popular morning trains sell out on weekends and holidays. If yours is full, the Yamabiko is the fallback, not the platform.
  • Say "Matsushimakaigan", not "Matsushima". Getting off at the wrong station costs you a 25-minute walk and a chunk of your afternoon.
  • Winter changes the timings. The last cruise leaves at 15:00, Zuiganji closes at 15:30 in December and January, and the Fukuura bridge shuts at 16:30. A winter day trip needs the early train, not the 08:18.
  • Check the boats before you go. Strong wind stops the cruise, and there is no point riding 40 minutes to a cancelled sailing.
