Green Cars on Tokyo's JR Lines: Worth the Extra Fare?

Rail guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: a Green Car is JR East's first-class seat on an otherwise ordinary commuter train: a wide reclining seat and a table in a double-decker car, for a surcharge of ¥750 to ¥1,550 on top of your normal fare. You don't reserve a seat — you buy a Green ticket, board cars 4 and 5, and sit in any free seat. For a day trip to Kamakura, Odawara or Atami, it's one of the best-value upgrades in Tokyo. For a ten-minute hop inside the city, it isn't.

If you've ever stood wedged into a packed train out of Tokyo, this is the better way to do the same journey — the quiet car in the middle of the train that most visitors walk straight past, because nobody explains how it works.

What a Green Car is — and where you'll find one

Green Cars run on JR East's medium-distance lines around Tokyo: the Tokaido, Yokosuka, Chuo (Rapid), Ome, Takasaki, Utsunomiya, Joban, Sobu (Rapid), Shonan-Shinjuku and Ueno-Tokyo lines. The Chuo Line Rapid and the Ome Line are the newest — Green Cars started there on 15 March 2025, when the trains grew from 10 cars to 12. You won't find them on the Yamanote Line, the Keihin-Tohoku Line, the Chuo-Sobu local trains or the subway: those are short-hop city trains, and the Green Car is built for longer runs out of the centre.

The cars themselves are easy to spot once you know what to look for. Each train has two of them, and they are almost always cars 4 and 5 — the double-decker cars in the middle of the formation, with a green stripe and a green four-leaf-clover mark. Inside, the upper and lower decks have forward-facing reclining seats with tables, and some cars also have free Wi-Fi and power outlets. The ends of the car are single-level, with overhead racks — but there is no dedicated space for oversized luggage, so if you're travelling with a big suitcase, read our luggage forwarding guide first.

Two things catch first-timers out. First, Green Car seats are non-reserved — you pay for the right to sit in the Green Car, not for a particular seat, so a seat is never guaranteed. Second, some trains on these lines run without Green Cars at all, so check the departure board before you wait at the wrong spot. It is also a different system from the Green Cars on shinkansen and limited express trains, where seats are reserved in advance.

What it costs

The Green fare is a surcharge on top of your normal train fare, and it depends only on how far you ride — not on the time of day. Buy it on a Suica before boarding and it's cheaper than buying a paper ticket or buying on the train.

Distance you rideSuica Green ticket (bought before boarding)Paper ticket / bought on board
Up to 50 km¥750¥1,010
Up to 100 km¥1,000¥1,260
101 km or more¥1,550¥1,810

Prices are JR East's published fares, at the time of writing (November 2026). On the Tokaido Line, for example: Tokyo to Yokohama is 28.8 km, so the Green surcharge is ¥750 with Suica; Tokyo to Odawara is 83.9 km, so it's ¥1,000; and Tokyo to Atami is 104.6 km, so it's ¥1,550. Remember, that's on top of the ordinary fare — the Green ticket alone doesn't get you through the ticket gates.

If you hold a rail pass, the same split applies. An Ordinary Japan Rail Pass covers the base fare but not the Green surcharge, so you still buy a Green ticket. A Green Car Japan Rail Pass covers Green Cars. If you're weighing passes for your whole trip, our rail pass guide does the maths.

How to buy a Green ticket and board

The cheapest way is the Suica Green ticket. At a station where Green Cars stop, use a ticket machine showing the transport IC logo: touch "Green Ticket", then "Suica Green Ticket", insert your Suica, and pick the line and station where you'll get off. You can also buy it in the Mobile Suica app or on Welcome Suica Mobile, without stopping at a machine. Suica, PASMO, Kitaca and TOICA all work; other IC cards don't.

A paper ticket is available at machines outside the gates or at a JR ticket office (Midori-no-Madoguchi), and from the attendant on board — but the paper fare applies even if you buy before boarding, so it always costs more than the Suica price. One Green ticket covers one person for one trip that day; if you don't use it, you can get a refund at a JR ticket office within the validity period.

Boarding looks like nothing happens, which is what confuses people. With a Suica Green ticket, sit down and touch your Suica to the reader above your seat. The little lamp above the seat changes from red (vacant) to green (occupied), and that's your check-in done. If you change seats on the same train, tap the reader at the new seat. If you transfer to another train partway, tap once before getting off and again after you board the next train. With a paper ticket, the rule is the other way round: seats with a red light above them are vacant, and the attendant will come round to check your ticket, so keep it handy.

One rule is absolute, and it surprises people: you need a Green ticket even if you're standing in the aisle or on the deck of a Green Car. If the Green Car is full and you'd rather move to an ordinary car, tell the Green attendant first.

When it's worth it — and when it isn't

The Green Car earns its money on rides of about 45 minutes or more, when the ordinary cars are standing-room-only. The classic visitor cases are all on the lines above: Tokyo to Kamakura on the Yokosuka Line (see our Kamakura day trip), Tokyo to Odawara for Hakone on the Tokaido Line, and Tokyo to Takao or Tachikawa on the Chuo Line Rapid. On a weekend, when day-trippers fill every seat, it is often the difference between a relaxing hour and an hour on your feet.

It isn't worth it for short hops inside Tokyo. Shinjuku to Shibuya or Tokyo to Yokohama is over in minutes, and the Green surcharge buys you almost nothing — save the ¥750 for the longer leg of the day. It's also a poor fit with a large suitcase (no dedicated luggage space), and it won't help on the Yamanote Line or the subway, which don't have Green Cars. And a seat is never guaranteed: at the busiest times, even the Green Car fills up.

One last point: the Green Car is not an express train. It stops at exactly the same stations as the ordinary cars, so it saves you no time — only the crowd. If speed is what you need, take the shinkansen or a limited express; our shinkansen guide covers those.

Finding the Green Car on the platform

On the platform, look for the car-number signs above the platform edge: the Green Cars stop at the marks for cars 4 and 5. The double-decker cars are visible from a long way off — taller than the rest of the train, with a green stripe along the side. Wait at the 4 or 5 marker and you'll board straight into the Green Car; wait anywhere else and you'll have to walk through the train to reach it.

At big stations, give yourself an extra minute or two to reach the right car — the platforms at Tokyo, Shinjuku and Yokohama are long and crowded. If you're not sure which platform you need, our guide to finding your platform explains the signs, line colours and station numbers. And if the train that arrives has no Green Cars, just take the next one — on the Tokaido and Chuo lines, another service is usually only minutes behind.
