Ekiben: Japan's Station Bento Boxes and Where to Buy Them

Food guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: an ekiben is a boxed meal sold at a station, made to be eaten on the train. Buy one before you board — the food cart is gone from ordinary shinkansen cars — at a dedicated ekiben shop inside the station, and eat it at your seat. Most boxes cost roughly ¥1,000–¥2,000 at the time of writing (November 2026), and each one tastes of the place it comes from: beef tongue in Sendai, trout sushi in Toyama, crab rice in Hokkaido.

What an ekiben actually is

The word is eki (station) plus bento (boxed meal). The first one is believed to have been sold at Utsunomiya Station in 1885: two rice balls and pickled daikon wrapped in bamboo leaves, for passengers on the slow trains of the time. As the railways spread, every region started selling a box built around its own specialty, and today there are around 2,000 varieties across Japan.

That regional link is the whole point. A konbini bento is the same everywhere; an ekiben is a small, edible postcard from the place you are leaving or passing through. The box itself is often part of it — a ceramic pot you keep, a wooden box, a container shaped like a crab or an octopus trap.

One thing first-timers get wrong: ekiben are made to be eaten cold, or at room temperature. There is no microwave on the train, and none is needed. The rice is seasoned, the fish and meat are cooked and marinated so they taste right without heat, and the box is designed not to leak in your bag. If it feels odd eating cold rice, trust the design — it works.

Where to buy one: Tokyo Station first

The best single place in Japan to buy an ekiben is Ekibenya Matsuri at Tokyo Station. It sits on the 1st-floor Central Passage, inside the JR ticket gates, and stocks more than 150 varieties from all over the country, plus the shop's own boxes and seasonal specials. It is open 5:30 to 22:00 every day, so it is there for the first shinkansen of the morning. Because it is inside the gates, you buy your ekiben after you have entered for your train — which is exactly the right order. You can pay with cash, a credit card, or an IC card such as Suica; see our IC card guide if you have not set one up yet.

Next door is its smaller sister counter, Ekibenya Odori (6:00 to 21:00), which sells Matsuri's best sellers for a faster choice when the main shop has a queue. If you are short on time, go there.

At other big stations the pattern is the same: a dedicated ekiben counter on the concourse or near the shinkansen gates. At Kyoto Station, head for Tabi Bento Ekiben Nigiwai Kyoto, on the 2nd-floor north–south concourse outside the west-exit ticket gates (7:00 to 21:30), which stocks boxes from across Japan alongside Kyoto's own bento. The department store food hall attached to the station — the depachika in the basement of JR Kyoto Isetan — is the fancier alternative. And at almost any shinkansen station, the platform kiosks sell a smaller range if you missed the main shop. Our Tokyo Station guide and Kyoto Station guide will help you find your way to the right floor.

If a box has no English label, look at the photo on the lid and the price — the picture is usually honest.

How to eat one on the train

Buy before you board. This matters more than it used to. Trolley sales on the Tokaido Shinkansen ended on 31 October 2023, so in an ordinary car there is no food cart coming down the aisle any more. Green Car passengers can still order drinks and snacks from their seat by phone, but everyone else is on their own — which is why the ekiben shops inside the station do such a roaring trade. See our shinkansen guide for how the seats and reservations work.

Eating is completely normal on the shinkansen and on reserved-seat limited expresses: the seats face forward, every seat has a fold-down tray table, and half the carriage will be unwrapping something on an intercity run. Eat soon after departure, once the train has settled. The shop gives you chopsticks, a wet towel and a small plastic bag with the box — use the bag for your rubbish, and take it off the train with you or use the bins on the platform. Do not leave it on the seat, and do not try to stuff it in the tiny bin in the toilet.

Two pieces of etiquette. First, do not eat on a crowded commuter train — the packed local lines with long bench seats and no tables are not the place for a meal, and the smell in a standing-room-only carriage is exactly what the unspoken rule is trying to spare everyone. Second, even on the shinkansen, skip anything with a very strong smell, like grilled fish with a heavy sauce, if the carriage is full. Most ekiben are mild by design, so this is rarely a problem.

Buy a drink with the box. The kiosks sell cold tea, coffee and beer, and a can of local beer or iced barley tea is the classic pairing. A bottle of water is worth having too — ekiben rice is seasoned and a little salty by design.

A few tips from the platform

  • Buy in the morning. Popular and seasonal boxes sell out, and the best ones are often gone by the afternoon. If you have a lunchtime train, buy the box when you arrive at the station, not five minutes before departure.
  • Check the expiry time on the lid. Every ekiben lists the hour it must be eaten by — usually the same day. That is the point of the box, and it is why you buy it for the train you are about to catch, not for tomorrow.
  • Keep the pot. If you buy Toge no Kamameshi, the ceramic pot is yours. Wash it at your hotel and it makes a fine rice bowl at home — or a souvenir that actually gets used.
  • Vegetarian and halal boxes exist but are scarce and rarely labelled in English. If you have a dietary restriction, a konbini onigiri with a clear label is the safer backup, and department store food halls have the widest choice.
  • One box per person is plenty. Ekiben look modest next to a restaurant lunch, but the rice is dense and the box is a full meal. Two people sharing one box and a couple of onigiri is the only way to taste more without wasting food.
