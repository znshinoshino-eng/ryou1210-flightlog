Sake Towns by Train: Fushimi and Saijo

Sightseeing guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: Fushimi in Kyoto and Saijo in Hiroshima are two of Japan's three great sake-brewing towns — the third is Nada, near Kobe — and both are easy half-day trips by train from a city you are probably visiting anyway. Fushimi has the museum, the canal and a tasting hall; Saijo has seven working breweries along one quiet street. You need to be 20 or older to taste, and the JR legs of both trips are covered if you already hold a Japan Rail Pass.

Both towns grew up around good brewing water, and both are walkable from the station — no car or tour bus needed for the walk itself.

Fushimi, Kyoto: the museum, the canal and the tasting hall

Fushimi sits south of central Kyoto, and it is the easiest sake town to reach: three different railways serve it, so you can come from whichever part of the city you are in. From Kyoto Station, take the Kintetsu Kyoto Line to Momoyama-Goryomae or the JR Nara Line to Momoyama. From Gion-Shijo or Sanjo, take the Keihan Main Line to Fushimi-Momoyama — and note that not every Keihan train stops there, so change at Tambabashi if yours does not. Do not confuse Fushimi-Momoyama with Fushimi-Inari, the station for the famous shrine: they are different stops, and the shrine is a few minutes north.

The one paid door in the district is the Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum, set in a brewery built in 1909. Gekkeikan itself was founded in 1637, and the museum walks you through its brewing tools, a short film on sake making and the old brewery building, ending with a tasting of three sakes. Admission is ¥600 for visitors aged 20 and over, ¥100 for ages 13 to 19, and free for 12 and under; visitors 13 and over get a souvenir, and the tasting is for 20 and over only. It opens 9:30 to 16:30, last admission 16:00, and a visit takes about 40 to 60 minutes. Two things catch people out: every payment in the museum is cashless only — no cash at all, since 1 July 2026 — and an online reservation, which opens two weeks ahead, gets you priority entry. Walk-ins are accepted but may have to wait. It is closed 13 to 16 August and 28 December to 4 January, plus occasional temporary closures, so check the museum's own site before you go. The museum is a 5-minute walk from Keihan Chushojima Station, 10 minutes from Keihan Fushimi-Momoyama or Kintetsu Momoyama-Goryomae, and 18 minutes from JR Momoyama.

The rest of the district is free to wander. The old warehouses line a quiet canal, and in the warmer months a small flat-bottomed boat, the Jukkokubune, runs along it past the warehouses — about 55 minutes, with the 2026 season running from March to early December, so check the current season before you go. Fushimi Sakagura Koji is a food hall where you can taste sake from breweries across the district in one place, and Gokonomiya Shrine, a few minutes from Fushimi-Momoyama, is known for its spring water. The 400-metre Otesuji shopping street runs through the middle of it all. Two hours covers the museum and the canal; half a day lets you add the boat, the shrine and a slow lunch.

Saijo, Hiroshima: seven breweries on one street

Saijo is the quieter of the two: the whole brewing district is one street, Sakagura-dori, within a 10-minute walk of JR Saijo Station. From Hiroshima Station, take a JR Sanyo Main Line local train east — about 40 minutes, and covered by the Japan Rail Pass. When you step out of the station, the tourist information centre on the second floor (9:00 to 18:00, English spoken) can point you to the street and tell you which breweries are open that day.

Seven breweries line the street, and most have a shop where you can taste and buy. Kamotsuru, founded in 1873, was a pioneer of daiginjo — the delicate, highly polished style — and has a small exhibition room and shop. Hakubotan is the oldest brewery in Saijo, brewing since 1675, and Kamoizumi, founded in 1912, was one of the first in Japan to brew only junmai sake, made from rice and koji alone; its retro western-style building now holds a café. The others — Sanyo Tsuru, Saijotsuru, Kirei and Fukubijin — each have their own shop, so the whole point of the visit is a slow afternoon on that street: taste a cup here and there, pick up a bottle to take home.

Come in October and the town changes completely. The Saijo Sake Festival runs every year on the weekend before Health and Sports Day, drawing 100,000 to 200,000 people over two days. The street fills with food stalls and stages, and a ticketed tasting plaza pours sake from breweries across Japan. The plaza tickets sell out, so buy them in advance on the festival's official site — and expect the trains to and from Hiroshima to be packed.

Tasting tips from a railway worker

  • The drinking age is 20. Japan's legal drinking age is 20, and the museum and festival tastings enforce it. Under-20s can still enjoy the museum and the street, just not the tasting.
  • Go slowly. Tastings are small cups, but seven breweries add up. Eat as you go — both towns have food stalls and restaurants in the district — and drink water between pours.
  • Plan your train home before the last tasting. Both towns are an easy ride back to the city, but the last local trains leave earlier than you might expect, and festival weekends are crowded. Check the return time when you arrive, not when you are ready to leave.
  • Buy the bottle, not the box set. The brewery shops sell bottles you will not find at the airport, often at the same price as in town. If you are flying home, pack bottles in checked luggage and check your airline's liquid rules first.
  • Ask what to try. Staff at the brewery shops are used to first-timers. Say whether you like it dry or sweet, light or rich, and they will pour accordingly — that is half the fun of going to the brewery instead of a department store.

Which town should you pick?

If you…Pick
Are based in Kyoto and have half a dayFushimi — the museum, the canal and the tasting hall fit in an afternoon, and you can pair it with Fushimi Inari
Are based in Hiroshima and want the quieter daySaijo — one street, seven breweries, and a town that still feels like a working brewery town
Are travelling in OctoberSaijo — the sake festival is worth planning a weekend around
Want the museum and the guided storyFushimi — Gekkeikan's museum is the best introduction to how sake is made
