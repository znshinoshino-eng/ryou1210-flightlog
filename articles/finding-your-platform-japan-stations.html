Finding Your Platform in a Big Japanese Station: Signs, Line Symbols and Station Numbers

Transit guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: before you set off, note three things: the line name, the direction your train is heading and the station number of where you're going, such as M17 or JY17. Inside the station, follow the signs for that line, then pick the platform whose sign shows your direction. If you get stuck, station staff and a few English-language help lines are there for exactly this.

Stations like Tokyo, Shinjuku and Osaka feel overwhelming the first time, mainly because several railway companies share one name. Once you know how the signs are built, it gets much easier. If you haven't yet read how the different companies fit together, start with how Tokyo's train network works.

Station numbers: the code that saves you

Tokyo Metro, Toei Subway and JR East in the Tokyo area, and JR West on its major lines in the Kinki (Kansai) area, give their stations a short code made of a line symbol plus a number. Japan's transport ministry, in its guideline on information for overseas visitors, notes that station numbering makes things more convenient for overseas travelers, and sets Japanese, English and pictograms as the basic trio for visitor information. A code like G09 is much easier to match on a sign than a long Japanese place name.

A few JR East line symbols you'll see a lot around Tokyo: JY Yamanote, JK Keihin-Tohoku, JC Chuo Rapid, JB Chuo-Sobu Local, JA Saikyo, JS Shonan-Shinjuku and JO Yokosuka/Sobu Rapid. At major transfer stations, JR East also shows a three-letter station code.

In the Kansai area, JR West notes that the company identifier "JR" is generally left off inside its own stations and trains, so at Osaka Station you'll mostly see "A47" rather than "JR-A47". JR West also says station numbers are only given on major lines within the Kinki area, so on smaller lines within the areas where it uses line symbols, you'll rely on the line symbol, color and station name.

Tip: when you plan a route, write down both the station name and its code. Tokyo Metro's own advice is to "remember the station name, number and line color".

Reading the signs, step by step

Big stations have a lot of signs, but they follow a pattern. Here's the order I'd use:

  1. Find the right company's entrance. One station name can be shared by several railway companies, so look for the operator you need. On Tokyo Metro, for example, you'll see the subway sign and the station name at each entrance, and then the line symbol and line name inside.
  2. Follow the line, not the station. Look for your line's symbol and color on the hanging signs. On Tokyo Metro, blue signs are the transfer guide and yellow signs are the exit guide, so follow blue when you're changing lines and yellow when you're leaving.
  3. Pick the direction. Tokyo Metro's advice is to "find your platform by looking for your train line and its direction on the sign." Check that your stop is in the direction shown, using the line map if you're unsure.
  4. Check the departure board. At stations with LED departure boards, JR West points out that you can check the destination, departure time and boarding locations, using the boards and the platform guide boards near the ticket gate.
  5. Confirm on board. As JR West puts it, in-car displays show the next stop for the current train, so you can spot a mistake at the next station.

On a JR West platform you can also double-check the station itself: JR West says at least one vertical station nameplate is generally located in front of every train car. In the areas where JR West uses line symbols, the line symbol and color for a train's route are also displayed on the outside of the train.

Official maps and apps that help

The single most useful thing before a big transfer is a look at the station's floor map. JR East's English site provides maps for most JR East stations (not every station is included). Shinjuku, for example, has English floor maps for 1F, 2F and B1F, which helps a lot when you're trying to work out which gate is closest to your platform.

Tokyo Metro has an official free app, Tokyo Metro For Tourists, available in English, Korean, Chinese (Simplified and Traditional), Thai, French and Spanish. For both of these you'll want mobile data in the station; if you haven't sorted that out yet, a Japan eSIM (affiliate link) is one option, rated 4.6 from 49.1K reviews on Klook at the time of writing. Asking for help (in English)

Asking is completely normal. JNTO's rail guide says staff are generally happy to help those who require assistance, and that ticket machines will typically have English menus. If your Japanese is limited, it helps to show staff the station name and code written down or on your phone. Here are the official options:

JR West doesn't list an English phone line on its inquiry page; it uses an English-only online inquiry form instead. In Kansai stations, ask staff at the ticket gate. If you leave something behind while rushing between platforms, see what to do if you lose something on a train.

Practical tips before a big transfer

  • Look at the floor map first. At big JR East stations like Tokyo or Shinjuku, a minute with the official map before you arrive saves a lot of wandering.
  • Avoid the crowds if you're carrying luggage. Signs are much harder to follow in a crowd. Our Tokyo rush hour guide has the times to avoid, and luggage forwarding lets you skip the problem entirely.
  • Riding the shinkansen? Our how to ride the shinkansen guide walks through tickets, gates and platforms.
  • Get your ticket sorted. An IC card means you don't need to work out fares at a machine for local trains. See Suica, PASMO and ICOCA explained.

Once you've done one big transfer, the pattern repeats everywhere: operator, line, direction, departure board.
