Train Delays in Japan: Refunds, Delay Certificates and What Staff Can Do

Practical guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked October 2026

Short answer: Japanese trains are usually on time, but weather, accidents and equipment trouble do happen. On JR, if a shinkansen or limited express arrives two hours or more late, the limited express charge is refunded in full. If your train is cancelled, you get a full refund, or a free ride back to where you started. Delay certificates are mostly for commuters and are now issued online. And if you're on a rail pass, a delay won't get you any money back or any extra days.

I work for a Japanese railway company, so I see the passenger side of delays every week. Here's what the official rules actually say, and how to get help without stress.

When a train is delayed or stopped: first steps

When something goes wrong, you'll usually hear announcements on the train and at the station, and departure boards will show delays or a suspension. Announcements in English can be short, so the practical routine is:

  • Stay calm and stay put if you're on a train stopped between stations. Don't try to leave the train by yourself; the crew will tell passengers what to do.
  • Check the operator's official website for train service information. This is where having mobile data really pays off (see eSIM and pocket WiFi in Japan).
  • Ask at the staffed window by the ticket gates, or at the ticket office for shinkansen and limited express tickets. Show your ticket and your destination written down or on your phone.
  • Keep your ticket, including the limited express ticket, until you've sorted out any refund.

From the railway side

During big disruptions the window gets busy. Have your ticket, your destination and the station you started from ready before you reach the front. It makes things much faster for you and for the staff.

JR refund rules for delays and cancellations

JR East and JR Central publish these rules on their official English pages. They cover ordinary tickets you bought at full price; for discounted tickets, JR Central says the refund depends on the ticket, so ask the staff.

What happenedWhat you get back (JR rules)
Trip cancelled before you start (train cancelled or service suspended)Full refund of the fare and charges
Train cancelled partway through your tripThe fare for the unused part, plus the limited express or express charge for the cancelled train
Train cancelled and you'd rather go backA free ride back to your departure station, and the fare and charges refunded in full
Shinkansen or limited express arrives two hours or more lateThe limited express or express charge refunded in full (the base fare is not refunded)
A late train makes you miss your connection, and you arrive two hours or more lateHandled the same way as a cancellation

A few details from JR East's page that are worth knowing:

  • You don't have to claim on the day. JR East accepts refund requests for cancellations and delays for up to one year from the day after the disruption.
  • Where to claim: a JR ticket office (Midori-no-madoguchi) or a travel agent.
  • Hotels and taxis aren't covered. JR East says it is not responsible for extra transport or hotel costs caused by delays.

JR Central also notes that if a limited express is cancelled partway, you may be able to continue on another limited express train, so ask the staff before you buy anything new.

The JR East page says "two hours or more", while JR Central's English page says "more than two hours". If your delay is right at two hours, ask at the ticket office.

Rail passes: no refunds and no extra days

This catches a lot of visitors out. The official JAPAN RAIL PASS site says the pass's validity is not extended and no refund is given because of suspended operations or delays, including weather, once you've started using the pass. JR East says the same for its own passes, such as the JR EAST PASS.

So on a pass, a delay costs you time, not money. If a storm is forecast, plan a buffer day before long shinkansen trips or your flight home. For a quick look at which passes make sense in the first place, see Japan Rail Passes: which ones are actually worth it.

Delay certificates (chien shomeisho)

A delay certificate (遅延証明書, chien shomeisho) is a note from the railway saying a line was running late. Japanese commuters use them to show school or work why they were late. As a visitor you rarely need one, but if someone asks you for proof that your train was late, this is it.

  • JR East posts printable certificates on its website for ordinary trains on its conventional lines. They cover delays of roughly 5 minutes or more, are shown by time band (for example 7:00–10:00), and stay online for 45 days. Limited express trains are not included.
  • Tokyo Metro also posts certificates by line and time band for delays of 5 minutes or more, with the past 35 days available. It has moved delay certificates online and no longer issues them on paper.

One thing to understand: JR East says a certificate shows the largest delay on that line during that time band. It doesn't show the delay of your particular train, and it doesn't prove you were on it. The pages are in Japanese; look for 遅延証明書 on the operator's site.

A delay certificate is not how you get a refund. Limited express refunds are handled at the ticket office with your ticket, as above.

What station staff can help with

In a disruption, the people at the staffed window or ticket office are your best resource. They can:

  • Tell you whether your line is running, and suggest another route or line to your destination
  • Handle refunds for cancelled trips and late limited express trains
  • Arrange your free ride back to your departure station if your trip is cancelled
  • Help with your shinkansen or limited express tickets if your train no longer runs as planned (see how to ride the shinkansen for how reserved and non-reserved seats work)

Riding on an IC card and not sure what happens to your fare after a disruption or a detour? Ask at the window rather than guessing at the gate. More on IC cards: Suica, PASMO and ICOCA explained.

Tips from someone who works on the railways

  • Leave a buffer on travel days. Don't plan a tight connection between a shinkansen and a flight. Weather delays are not refunded on passes, and extra hotel or taxi costs aren't covered either.
  • Other operators have their own rules. Private railways and subways set their own refund policies. When in doubt, ask at that company's window.
  • Screenshot your booking. If you have to queue at a ticket office, having your train number, seat and route on your phone saves time.
