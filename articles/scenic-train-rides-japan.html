Scenic Train Rides in Japan Worth the Trip

Sightseeing guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: Japan's best scenic trains are worth planning a whole day around, not squeezing in between other sights. Four stand out: Kyoto's Sagano Romantic Train, a short gorge ride you can pair with a river boat; the Resort Shirakami, a full day along the Sea of Japan coast; the Kurobe Gorge Railway, an open-air ride into the Japan Alps; and the Oykot, a slow trip through rice fields and river valleys in Nagano. All four need advance booking, none is covered by the Japan Rail Pass on its own, and each runs on a seasonal calendar — check the operator's site before you build your day around it.

Sagano Romantic Train: Kyoto's gorge ride

This is the easiest of the four to fit into a first trip. The little trolley train runs 7.3 km through the Hozu Gorge between Torokko Saga, next to JR Saga-Arashiyama station, and Torokko Kameoka, in about 25 minutes. The river runs below, forested cliffs rise on both sides, and the train is slow enough to actually look at it all. There are four stations, and the one-way fare is the same whichever section you ride: ¥880 for adults and ¥440 for children at the time of writing.

Every seat is reserved, so book online — sales open at midnight Japan time one month before the travel date, and autumn weekends sell out fast. Same-day tickets are sold at Torokko Saga, Torokko Arashiyama and Torokko Kameoka stations while seats remain, but not at Torokko Hozukyo. Car 5 is the open-air car, with no window glass: wonderful on a fine day, wet in the rain. The classic way to do it is to ride the train one way to Kameoka and come back down the same river by Hozugawa River boat (affiliate link) to Arashiyama. You can also book Sagano Romantic Train tickets on Klook (affiliate link) if you prefer to sort it out before you travel.

The train runs from 1 March to 29 December, is closed in January and February, and is usually closed on Wednesdays in season. Note the change coming: the current cars retire after 29 December 2026, and a new train starts on 1 March 2027 with a ¥620 seat fee on top of the fare (adult total ¥1,500). The Japan Rail Pass does not cover this train, even though JR gets you to Saga-Arashiyama station.

Resort Shirakami: a day on the Gono Line coast

The Resort Shirakami is the opposite of the Sagano ride: a whole day, not half an hour. It runs between Akita and Aomori along the Gono Line, right beside the Sea of Japan for long stretches, with the Shirakami Mountains on the other side. The full run takes about 5 hours 15 minutes — train No. 1 leaves Akita at 08:19 and reaches Aomori at 13:34. There are three train sets, the green Buna, the blue Aoike and the red-striped Kumagera, all with big windows, wood-panelled interiors and box seats. On some days there are shamisen performances and local events on board, and most trains stop for about 15 minutes at Senjojiki so you can step out onto the rocky shore.

All seats are reserved. You need a basic fare ticket plus a reserved-seat ticket: the seat charge for the full Akita–Aomori run is ¥840 for adults at the time of writing. If you hold a Japan Rail Pass or a JR East Pass that covers the area, the seat reservation itself is free — you still have to make it. Reservations open at 10:00 one month before the travel date, on JR East's Eki-net site or at a JR ticket office. Up to three trains a day run each way in the main season, but from December to March the train runs on selected days only, mostly at weekends. This is also the ride most likely to be stopped by weather: strong wind and high waves on this coast can cancel it, so leave slack at both ends of the day. The Juniko lakes, a popular stop off the line, are open roughly April to November.

Kurobe Gorge Railway: the torokko into the Alps

The Kurobe Gorge Railway is an open-air trolley train that runs 20.1 km from Unazuki, in Toyama, deep into the Kurobe Gorge to Keyakidaira, crossing more than 20 bridges above turquoise water. The ride takes about 80 minutes each way. The adult one-way fare is ¥2,480 (children ¥1,240) at the time of writing, so a return is ¥4,960; a windowed Relax car costs ¥600 extra. The railway is private, so the Japan Rail Pass does not cover it — but holders of selected JR passes get 10% off a same-day return ticket bought at Unazuki.

The 2026 season is a special one to get right. The January 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake damaged a bridge and cut the line short for more than two years; the full line to Keyakidaira only reopened on 1 October 2026. The 2026 season runs to 30 November, with a short Unazuki–Sasadaira shuttle only from 1 to 6 December, and the 2027 season dates had not been published when this was written. Even now, access at Keyakidaira is limited to the station building, its rooftop viewpoint and the visitor centre — the gorge walks beyond it are still closed for repairs. Book online from three months ahead until two days before travel, or buy same-day tickets at Unazuki from 07:40 if seats remain. To get there, take the Hokuriku Shinkansen to Kurobe-Unazukionsen, then the Toyama Chiho Railway to Unazuki-Onsen and walk about five minutes to the gorge railway's station.

Oykot: slow travel on the Iiyama Line

The Oykot is the quietest ride on this list, and that is the point. Its name is "Tokyo" spelled backwards: a deliberate antidote to the capital, running along the Chikuma River between Nagano and Tokamachi on the Iiyama Line. The two-car train takes about two and a half hours through rice fields, forests and mountain valleys, with an interior designed to feel like a traditional Japanese home — wood floors, box seats with tables, old-fashioned curtains. On some days local musicians perform on board and you can taste regional snacks and sake.

Like the Shirakami, the Oykot is reserved seats only: you need a fare ticket plus a reserved-seat ticket, and pass holders reserve the seat free. Reservations open at 10:00 one month before the travel date. It runs mainly on weekends and holidays, with a winter version called the Tokamachi Yukimi Oykot when the snow comes. There is no need to rush this one — the ride is the destination, so plan it as a slow day out from Nagano. If you get off at Iiyama, look for the karakuri clock at the station, which performs on the hour.

Booking rules that apply to all four

  • Book the moment sales open. The Sagano train opens at midnight one month ahead; the two JR trains open at 10:00 one month ahead; Kurobe opens three months ahead. Autumn weekends and cherry-blossom season go first.
  • Budget for the seat, not just the fare. The Sagano and Kurobe prices are all-in. On the JR joyful trains, the fare ticket and the reserved-seat ticket are separate — and a rail pass covers the fare, not the reservation, though the reservation is free for pass holders.
  • Check the season, not just the timetable. Sagano closes in January and February, Kurobe closes for winter, and the Shirakami barely runs from December to March. A train that exists in October may not exist in February.
  • Build in slack. Coastal wind can stop the Shirakami, and a sold-out Sagano train has no standing room to fall back on. If the ride is the reason for the day trip, keep the rest of the day loose.
  • Getting there is part of the cost. None of these trains starts in central Tokyo. You will take a shinkansen or limited express to Kyoto, Akita, Toyama or Nagano first — see our shinkansen guide and our rail pass guide to work out whether a pass pays for the journey to the start of the ride.
