Rush Hour in Tokyo: Times to Avoid, Women-Only Cars and Train Etiquette

Practical guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked October 2026

Short answer: on weekdays, Tokyo's trains are at their most crowded roughly between 7:20 and 9:00 a.m. on trains heading into the city centre. If you can, start your sightseeing after about 9:30. If you do ride at rush hour, know where the women-only cars are, take your backpack off, and stand clear of the doors when people need to get out.

I work for a Japanese railway company, and the morning rush is the one part of Tokyo's network I'd steer a first-time visitor around. It isn't dangerous, but it is tiring with luggage, and a little planning makes a big difference.

When is rush hour in Tokyo?

Every year Japan's transport ministry (MLIT) publishes how crowded each major line is during its busiest hour. For the 2025 survey (fiscal year 2025), the average across the Tokyo area's main sections was 143% of capacity, up from 139% the year before. On the busiest sections, the worst hour falls between about 7:20 and 9:00 a.m., depending on the line:

Two things to notice. First, every busiest section in the table is on the way into central Tokyo. In my experience, if your hotel is in the centre and you're heading out of town early, your train may be much calmer. Second, the women-only cars below run on weekdays, and in my experience weekend mornings are a much easier time to ride with luggage.

Tokyo Metro also says that on the Hibiya Line, morning riders concentrate especially between Minami-Senju and Iriya, and that the Tokyo Metro my! app shows how crowded each car is. That's handy if you want to pick a quieter car.

Women-only cars: which lines, which times

Several Tokyo lines set aside one car as women-only during the weekday morning rush (and on the Saikyo Line, late at night too). The car is marked with stickers on the platform and on the train windows. These are the main ones a visitor is likely to use:

Tokyo Metro's Tozai Line also has a women-only car on trains towards Nakano between Nishi-Funabashi and Otemachi, and the Oedo Line has one on some trains from Tochomae towards Iidabashi. The exact rules are on each operator's page, and all three operators say the cars may not run when the timetable is disrupted.

Who can ride? Besides women, the operators say elementary-school-age children and younger, passengers with disabilities, and the people assisting them can also use these cars. Toei adds that some passengers have disabilities you can't see. The system relies on everyone's cooperation rather than enforcement, but as a visitor, the polite thing for men is simply to choose another car during those hours.

For women travelling alone, these cars are a calmer option in the morning. Outside the listed times, they're ordinary cars open to everyone.

The etiquette locals actually notice

Every year the Japan Private Railway Association asks people which behaviour on trains and at stations bothers them most. In its 2025 survey (5,202 people answered), the top answers were:

  1. Coughing or sneezing without covering up (34.7%)
  2. How people sit: not moving up, stretching legs out (31.9%)
  3. Loud conversation and rowdy behaviour (30.2%)
  4. Standing around near the doors (27.6%)
  5. Smartphone use, such as walking while looking at your phone (21.6%)

On luggage, the single behaviour people found most annoying was wearing a bag on your back (27.7%), ahead of wet or badly held umbrellas (18.2%) and holding a bag in front of your body (17.6%). Carry-on suitcases came next (10.4%). On a full train, a backpack takes up the space of another person, so take it off and hold it low, or put it on the overhead rack.

Tokyo Metro's own manners page adds a few more:

  • Phones on silent, no calls on the train. Near the priority seats during rush hour and other busy times, Tokyo Metro asks people to switch phones off.
  • Priority seats are for older passengers, people with disabilities, people with small children and pregnant women. You may see a "Maternity Mark" keyring on a bag; Tokyo Metro asks passengers to be considerate towards mothers wearing one.
  • Don't run for the train. Another one will come soon, and running onto a closing door is dangerous.
  • Strollers: parents are asked not to block the way, and other passengers are asked to be understanding.
  • No smoking anywhere in the stations.

Riding a packed train: a railway worker's tips

  • Let people off first. Stand to the side of the doors on the platform and wait for passengers to get off before boarding. On a full train, if you're by the door and people behind you need to get out, step out onto the platform, let them pass, then get back on.
  • Move into the car. Don't stop just inside the doors; move towards the middle so others can board.
  • Check your exit before you board. Knowing your stop in advance saves a last-second push. Our guide to Tokyo's train network explains how lines and transfers fit together.
  • Pick the end cars. In my experience, the cars nearest the stairs at big stations fill first. Walking a few cars along the platform often gets you more space.
  • Top up your IC card beforehand. A queue at the fare adjustment machine at 8:30 a.m. is no fun. See our IC card guide.

Arriving or leaving with suitcases during rush hour

This is where rush hour really matters for visitors. A large suitcase on a packed commuter train is hard on you and on everyone around you. A few ways around it:

  • Time your trip. If your flight lands early in the morning, a coffee at the airport and a train after 9:30 is often the better choice. Our guides to Narita to Tokyo and Haneda to Tokyo compare the trains and buses.
  • Use a reserved-seat option. A reserved seat on an airport express means you're not standing in a commuter crowd with your bag; the airport guides above list which services have them.
  • Send your suitcase ahead. Luggage delivery lets you ride with just a day bag. See our luggage forwarding guide, or leave bags in a station locker with our coin locker guide.
  • Take a car. With a family or lots of bags, a pre-booked transfer means a driver meets you at arrivals and you skip the trains completely. Keep in mind that roads into the city are busy in the morning too.

Simple ways to dodge the crowds

  • Start sightseeing after 9:30 a.m. Have breakfast near your hotel and let the commuters go first.
  • Walk short hops. Some central stations are close together, and a short walk can beat a packed two-stop ride.
  • Plan big station transfers for midday. Huge stations like Shinjuku and Tokyo Station are far easier to navigate when they're not at their busiest.
  • If the line stops, trains can get even more crowded than usual. Our guide to train delays explains what staff can help with.

Tokyo's rush hour looks intimidating in photos, but it runs like clockwork. Avoid the busiest hour when you can, keep your bag low, and let people off first, and you'll fit right in.
