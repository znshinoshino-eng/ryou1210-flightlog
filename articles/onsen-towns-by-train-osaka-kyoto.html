Onsen Towns by Train from Osaka and Kyoto: Arima, Kinosaki and Shirahama

Day trip guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: for a quick soak, go to Arima — about an hour from Osaka, with two public baths you can walk into without a ryokan booking. For the classic onsen town, go to Kinosaki and stay the night: about 2 hours 45 minutes from Osaka, and the whole town is built around strolling between seven public baths in a yukata. For sea air with your soak, go to Shirahama, about 2 hours 30 minutes south, where the oldest bath sits in the rocks at the edge of the Pacific. And if you do Kinosaki or Shirahama and back, one rail pass pays for the whole trip.

Arima and Shirahama are both counted among Japan's three oldest hot-spring towns, alongside Dogo in Ehime. Prices below are at the time of writing (November 2026) and can change.

Arima Onsen: the closest soak

Arima sits in the mountains behind Kobe, about an hour from Osaka Station according to the town's tourism association. The simplest way is the direct Hankyu highway bus from the Hankyu Sanbangai terminal in Umeda, about an hour with no changes. By train, take a JR train to Kobe-Sannomiya (about 20 minutes), then the subway to Tanigami (¥280) and the Kobe Electric Railway to Arima Onsen Station (¥440), changing at Arimaguchi — about 30 minutes from Sannomiya. From Kyoto, a highway bus runs from Kyoto Station in about 1 hour 40 minutes, or come via Osaka and pick up the Umeda bus.

Arima's two public baths are run by the town, and you can try both without staying anywhere:

  • Kin no Yu (the gold bath): ¥800 for adults, ¥650 on weekdays (excluding busy holiday periods). Open 8:00–22:00, last entry 21:30. Closed on the 2nd and 4th Tuesday of the month and on 1 January. The water is kinsen — iron-rich and salt-rich, reddish-brown, with about 1.5 to 2 times the salt content of seawater.
  • Gin no Yu (the silver bath): ¥700 for adults, ¥550 on weekdays. Open 9:00–21:00, last entry 20:30. Closed on the 1st and 3rd Tuesday and on 1 January. The water is ginsen — clear, carbonated spring water.
  • Both together: a 2-bath ticket costs ¥1,200 for adults. The two baths close on alternating Tuesdays, so on any given Tuesday one of them is almost always open — but check the day before you go.

There is also a free foot bath next to Kin no Yu if you only want a taste. If you are basing yourself in Osaka, see our where to stay in Osaka guide.

Kinosaki Onsen: the classic overnight

Kinosaki is the town the phrase "onsen town" was made for: seven public bathhouses (sotoyu) along a willow-lined canal, with guests walking between them in yukata and wooden geta after dark. From Shin-Osaka or Osaka Station, the limited express Kounotori runs direct to Kinosaki Onsen Station in about 2 hours 45 minutes; the ordinary reserved-seat fare is about ¥6,140 in the normal season. From Kyoto Station, the limited express Kinosaki takes about 2 hours 20 minutes, for about ¥5,500. Kounotori trains are all-reserved, so book a seat before you board — you cannot just turn up with an IC card.

The bathing system is simple once you know it:

  • Seven baths, six open. Satono-yu, the bath by the station, has been closed since April 2024 for rebuilding. The other six are open, each with its own weekly closing day, and two of them (Mandara-yu and Yanagi-yu) only open from 15:00.
  • Yumepa day pass: ¥1,500 for adults and ¥750 for children, for unlimited entry to every open bath that day. A single bath is ¥800, so the pass pays off from your second bath. If you stay at a ryokan in town, the pass is usually included with your room, valid until 13:00 on checkout day.
  • Tattoos: all of Kinosaki's public baths accept tattooed guests, which the town's official tourism site states outright — unusual in Japan, and worth knowing if tattoos have kept you out of other baths.

A day trip is possible, but you miss the point of the place: the baths that open at 15:00, the lantern-lit canal after dark, and the slow morning after a ryokan breakfast are the reason to stay. Winter is snow-crab season, and the ropeway up Mt. Daishi is the easy viewpoint over the town.

Shirahama: beach town with an ocean bath

Shirahama, on Wakayama's Pacific coast, pairs a white-sand beach with hot springs that claim more than 1,300 years of history. From Shin-Osaka or Tennoji, the limited express Kuroshio runs direct to Shirahama Station in about 2 hours 30 minutes; the ordinary reserved-seat fare is about ¥6,140 in the normal season. Shirahama Station is not in the town centre — take a Meiko Bus from the station to the beach area, about 10 to 15 minutes.

  • Saki-no-yu: the bath to plan around. Open-air rock pools right on the shoreline, where the waves break metres away. Admission is ¥500. Hours vary by season — 8:00–17:00 from October to March, 8:00–18:00 in April to June and September, and 7:00–19:00 in July and August. There are no showers and no soap is allowed, and the bath can close when the sea is rough, so check conditions locally before you go.
  • Besides the bath: Shirarahama Beach, Engetsu Island's rock arch at sunset, and the Sandanbeki cliffs. Most hotels also sell day-use bathing if you want a proper indoor bath with showers.

A day trip works if you catch a morning Kuroshio and an evening one back, but like Kinosaki it is better with a night.

The pass that pays for itself

If your trip includes Kinosaki or Shirahama, check the pass math before buying single tickets. The JR Kansai Wide Area Pass costs ¥12,000 for adults (¥6,000 for children) for 5 consecutive days, and JR West lists reserved seats on the Kounotori and Kuroshio limited expresses, the Haruka airport express and the Sanyo Shinkansen between Shin-Osaka and Okayama as covered. One round trip between Shin-Osaka and Kinosaki Onsen costs ¥12,280 in ordinary reserved seats — already more than the pass.

The pass is only for visitors with a foreign passport entering as a Temporary Visitor; buy it from JR West and reserve your seats before boarding. Note that the cheaper Kansai Area Pass is gone — JR West ended its sales on 30 September 2026 — so do not plan around it from old guides. If you would rather have the pass on your phone before you travel, the Kansai Wide Area Pass on Klook (affiliate link) was rated 4.8 from about 10,800 reviews and showed from US$75.09 at the time of writing; prices vary by option. Onsen manners in two minutes

These come from JNTO's bathing guide:

  • Wash first. "You are expected to thoroughly wash your whole body before entering the hot spring." Use the soap and shampoo at the washing stations and rinse off completely.
  • Leave swimwear in the changing room. "Onsen bathing is typically enjoyed in the nude, so clothing (including swimwear) should be left in the changing area."
  • Keep the small towel out of the water. Fold it and put it on your head or on the side of the bath. Tie up long hair or wrap it in a towel.
  • Tattoos: "Some traditional onsen may not accept people with a tattoo, but this is beginning to change." Kinosaki's baths all accept them; Arima's two public baths publish no tattoo policy, so ask before you go. A private bath (kashikiri) rented for your group is another option.
  • No photos in the bathing area, and avoid alcohol before bathing.

Practical tips from the railway side

  • Reserve the limited express. Kounotori trains are all-reserved, and Kuroshio reserved seats sell out around holidays. Book when you buy the pass or ticket, not at the platform.
  • Check the closing day before you go. Every Kinosaki bath closes one weekday, Arima's two baths alternate Tuesdays, and Saki-no-yu can close for rough seas. A five-minute check saves a wasted trip.
  • Travel light for one night. Send your big suitcase on to your next hotel with luggage forwarding, or leave it in a station locker, and take a small overnight bag.
  • Carry cash for the small baths. Saki-no-yu takes ¥500 at a ticket machine, and small-town baths are not the place to count on a card. See our IC card guide for where cards do and do not work.
