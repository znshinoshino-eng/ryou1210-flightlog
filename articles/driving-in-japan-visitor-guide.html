Driving in Japan: Permits, Rules and When to Take the Train

Transit guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: most visitors drive on a paper International Driving Permit (IDP) issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention, obtained at home before the trip. Drivers from Switzerland, Germany, France, Belgium, Monaco and Taiwan use their home licence plus an official Japanese translation instead. And for the big cities and the classic city-to-city routes, the train is usually faster and cheaper — a car earns its keep in Hokkaido, Okinawa and the countryside, where trains don't reach.

The licence you need — and the one that doesn't work

Japan only recognises IDPs issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention on Road Traffic. A permit issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention is not valid here, even if your country signed both treaties — so check which convention your permit names on the cover before you travel. You must get the permit in your own country, from the official issuing authority, usually your national automobile association. You cannot obtain one after you arrive in Japan.

It also has to be the paper booklet. Card-type and smartphone versions of IDPs are not valid in Japan, according to JNTO. Carry three things whenever you drive: the IDP, your original home licence, and your passport.

Two clocks run on your permit. It is valid for up to one year from the date it was issued, and you may only drive in Japan for up to one year from the date you arrived, whichever is shorter. Your arrival date is confirmed by the entry stamp in your passport, so if you pass through an automated gate at the airport, go to a staffed gate or the customs desk and get the stamp — rental companies check it. If your home country doesn't issue permits in the 1949 Geneva format at all, the permit route is closed to you, so check with your national automobile association before you book.

The six countries that need a translation instead

Licences from Switzerland, Germany, France, Belgium, Monaco and Taiwan work differently. These countries don't issue 1949 Geneva-format permits, so their drivers carry their original licence plus an official Japanese translation, which lets them drive for one year from their arrival in Japan.

The best-known issuer is the Japan Automobile Federation (JAF): ¥6,000 at the time of writing. Two catches: JAF only accepts applications online, and its site only works from a network inside Japan — so you apply after you arrive, not before you fly. The finished translation is printed at a convenience store in Japan; allow a few working days, and plan your first days without a car. Translations from your country's embassy or consulate in Japan are also accepted; a translation by any other translator is not.

Renting the car

At the counter, the rental company will check your passport, your home licence and your IDP or translation together — arrive without one of the three and there is no car. The big companies with English booking are Toyota Rent a Car, Nippon Rent-A-Car, Times Car Rental, Orix, Nissan and JR Rent-A-Car, with desks at airports and outside most major stations.

JNTO's average rates for a 24-hour rental with unlimited mileage are about ¥5,000–11,100 for a sub-compact and ¥9,000–13,500 for a compact at the time of writing, varying by company and season. Fuel is extra — about ¥170 a litre in JNTO's guide figure — and the car goes back with a full tank. Rental companies require insurance with a certain level of coverage, so check what is included, and walk around the car with the staff to note scratches or dents before you drive off.

Ask for English navigation when you book, and reserve a child seat at the same time if you need one. Most Japanese rental cars are automatics, and the built-in navigation accepts a phone number or a Mapcode for the destination, so keep your hotel's phone number handy.

The road rules that catch visitors out

Japan drives on the left, with the steering wheel on the right. The default speed limits are 60 km/h on ordinary roads and 100 km/h on expressways when no sign shows a different limit — and signs often do show a lower one, so follow the posted number.

  • Seatbelts for everyone, front and back. Children under six must ride in a child seat.
  • No alcohol at all. Do not drive after drinking — the penalties are severe.
  • No phone use while driving. Set the route before you move.
  • Stop at railway crossings. Come to a full stop at the stop line and check both ways before crossing, even when the barriers are up — and yield to pedestrians at crossings.
  • Parking is scarce in city centres. Only park in designated spaces; parking elsewhere can bring a fine. In snowy areas, fit winter tyres or chains.

Tolls, ETC and the expressway passes

Most expressways charge tolls, and they add up fast. JNTO's example is about ¥10,070 in tolls for one route from Tokyo to Kyoto — before fuel, rental and parking. At the gates, lanes with a green sign take cash or a credit card; lanes with a purple sign are for ETC, Japan's electronic toll system. Rent an ETC card from the rental company; the tolls are settled when you return the car. Some gates are now ETC-only, with no cash lane.

If you are touring one region by car, ask about the regional expressway passes sold only to international travellers. They give unlimited expressway use inside one region for a fixed number of days — useful in Hokkaido, Tohoku or Kyushu, where the driving distances are long. The old nationwide Japan Expressway Pass stopped selling in December 2023, so ignore older guides that recommend it.

The same logic holds inside the cities. In Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, trains and subways reach everything, traffic is slow, and parking is hard to find. If your trip mixes a city stay with a driving leg, pick the car up on the day you leave town — rental desks sit outside major stations, so you don't have to drive in the city at all. And if you'd rather not drive but still want a car with a driver, see our guide to private car transfers and chauffeur hire in Tokyo.

The car earns its keep where the trains thin out. JNTO puts it plainly: the islands of Hokkaido and Okinawa are much more accessible by car, and the same is true of rural Honshu, Kyushu and Shikoku — mountain roads and onsen towns that see a couple of buses a day, if that. That is the trip to rent for: ride the train to the region, hire the car locally, and give it back before the city leg.

Before you get behind the wheel

  • 1949 Geneva IDP in paper-booklet form, plus your home licence and passport — or, for the six translation countries, the original licence plus the official translation.
  • An entry stamp in your passport, from a staffed gate.
  • Child seat reserved with the car if anyone in your party is under six.
  • ETC card rented with the car, and English navigation requested at booking.
  • Fuel type noted, and a petrol station near the return point — the car goes back with a full tank.
  • Winter tyres or chains if your route goes into snowy country.

Sort the paperwork at home, hire the car where the trains stop being useful, and let the shinkansen do the long city-to-city legs.
