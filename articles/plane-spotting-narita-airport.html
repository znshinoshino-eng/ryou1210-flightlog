Plane Spotting at Narita: The Best Public Spots

Spotting guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: Narita is one of the easiest airports in the world for plane spotting: free observation decks in Terminals 1 and 2, two public hill parks at opposite ends of the main runway where wide-body jets pass low overhead, and an aviation museum with its own rooftop deck. You do not need a car or a boarding pass for any of them — every spot is reachable by train and bus — but the wind decides which end of the runway is in use, so check it before you pick your spot.

The two runways, and why the wind picks your spot

Narita has two runways. Runway A (16R/34L) is 4,000 metres long and takes most departures, plus the A380s and heavy freighters. Runway B (16L/34R) is 2,500 metres and mostly handles arrivals, especially in the morning. Aircraft take off and land into the wind, so the wind decides which end of Runway A is busy. In winter, northerly winds are common and aircraft land from the south, over Hikoki no Oka; in summer, southerly winds bring landings from the north, over Sakura no Yama. That is the whole trick to Narita spotting: check the wind, then go to the end the planes are coming from.

The free observation decks in Terminals 1 and 2

Start here if you are already at the airport. The Terminal 1 deck is on the 5th floor and looks out across the full length of the 4,000-metre runway, so this is the deck for watching real take-offs and landings rather than parked planes. Terminal 2 has two decks on the 4th floor, one on each side of the building, and the planes sit parked right in front of you — a good close-up of fuselages and ground crews at work. Both decks are free and open 06:30 to 21:00 from April to September, and 07:00 to 21:00 from October to March, and they can close in strong wind or heavy rain. The decks are landside, so you do not need a boarding pass — they work for a long layover as well as for a dedicated trip. Terminal 3 has no deck of its own; follow the connecting walkway over to Terminal 2. Two things to know before you set up: photography with tripods, stepladders or large equipment is prohibited on the raised Mountain Deck section of the Terminal 1 deck, and the coin telescopes on the decks cost ¥100 for 120 seconds. Sakura no Yama: the hill park at the north end

Sakura no Yama is the classic Narita spotting spot. It is a small hill park just north of Runway A, where aircraft on approach pass close overhead — close enough that a phone camera catches the airline livery clearly. About 500 cherry trees are planted on the hill, so in late March and early April it doubles as one of the most unusual cherry-blossom spots in the Tokyo area — and gets very crowded. The park is free and open 06:00 to 23:00; the Sora no Eki Sakura-kan shop inside sells local vegetables and souvenirs, open 09:00 to 17:00. Pick this spot when the wind is from the south, more common in summer: aircraft land towards you from the north, and on a north-wind day they take off towards the hill instead. Getting there is easy from the airport: a bus leaves from the 1st floor of Terminal 2 and takes about 15 minutes to the Sakura no Yama stop, for ¥210 at the time of writing. From Narita town, the community bus from the east exit of Keisei Narita Station takes about 15 minutes and costs ¥200 for adults. Hikoki no Oka and the Museum of Aeronautical Sciences: the south end

Hikoki no Oka — "Airplane Hill" — is Sakura no Yama's counterpart at the south end of Runway A, about 600 metres from the runway. An open grassy park with three gentle hills, it is free to enter and open 06:00 to 23:00, with a heart-shaped monument on the southern hilltop that has become a popular photo spot. Pick this end when the wind is from the north, more common in winter: aircraft on approach pass almost directly overhead. Vending machines and toilets are on site, with 93 free parking spaces. The park is about a 10-minute walk from the Museum of Aeronautical Sciences, and the two make a natural pair. The museum sits right under the flight path, with a 5th-floor observation room and a rooftop deck looking straight onto take-offs and landings, around 20 real aircraft outside — some you can board — and flight simulators inside. Admission is ¥900 for adults, ¥400 for junior and senior high school students, and ¥300 for children aged 4 and over, at the time of writing. It is open 10:00 to 17:00, last admission 16:30, and closed on Mondays (the next day if Monday is a public holiday). The museum has announced a closure for equipment renewal in December 2026, reopening planned from January 2027 — check its site before a December visit. To reach both, take the bus from stop 30 on the 1st floor of Terminal 1, or stop 2 on the 1st floor of Terminal 2 — about ¥210–280 one way, roughly once an hour, so check the timetable before you commit.

Sanrizuka Sakura no Oka: the quieter hill for Runway B

If the main runway is quiet or you want Runway B arrivals, there is a smaller hill park on the south side of the airport: Sanrizuka Sakura no Oka. It has 114 cherry trees, benches and toilets, and a clear view of aircraft using Runway B. It is the least crowded of the three hill parks; the trade-off is access: a JR bus from JR Narita Station towards Yokaichiba, about 30 minutes, get off at Ieiriguchi and walk about 10 minutes. It is a good second stop if you are already in Narita town for the day — Naritasan Shinshoji Temple and its 800-metre food street are about 10 minutes on foot from JR Narita Station.

Planning your visit

  • Check the wind first. A quick look at the forecast tells you which end of Runway A is in use: northerly wind means Hikoki no Oka, southerly wind means Sakura no Yama. The terminal decks work in either wind, so they are the safe choice if you cannot choose.
  • Runway B takes most of the morning arrivals. If you arrive early, the Terminal 2 decks and Sanrizuka Sakura no Oka catch that wave; the big departures on Runway A build up through the afternoon.
  • The hill parks have little shade. Bring water and sun protection in summer, and something warm in winter — both parks are open grass and the wind comes straight off the runway.
  • Leave the tripod at home for the Terminal 1 deck. Tripods, stepladders and large equipment are prohibited on the raised section, and the fence has camera holes to shoot through instead.
  • Keep an eye on the time. The decks close at 21:00 and the parks at 23:00, but evening buses back to the terminals and Narita Station are infrequent — check the return timetable when you arrive, not when you are ready to leave.
  • Pair it with Haneda. If you enjoy this, Haneda has three free observation decks of its own, and the same wind rule applies. See our Haneda spotting guide and our guide to reading runway direction.
