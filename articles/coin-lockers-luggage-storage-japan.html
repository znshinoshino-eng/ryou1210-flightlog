Coin Lockers and Luggage Storage at Japanese Stations: Sizes, Fees and Time Limits

Practical guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked October 2026

Short answer: almost every sizeable station in Japan has coin lockers, and they're the easiest way to sightsee on a check-out day. Small and medium lockers are cheap and plentiful; the tall lockers that fit a big suitcase are few and go first. Lockers are for short stays (often up to 3 days), and after that your bag is taken away to storage and costs extra to get back. If you have a large suitcase, a busy station or a fixed plan, reserve a locker or use a staffed storage counter instead of hoping one is free.

I work for a Japanese railway company, and "where can I leave my suitcase?" is one of the questions station staff hear most. Here's how it actually works.

How a station coin locker works

Lockers are usually in banks near the ticket gates, some inside the gates and some outside. The basic routine:

  1. Find an empty locker of the right size and put your bag in.
  2. Close the door and pay at the panel. Many newer lockers take IC cards such as Suica or PASMO, and some also take credit cards or QR-code payments.
  3. Keep whatever the locker gives you or tells you to use to open it again (a key, a number or code), and follow the instructions on the panel or door when you collect.

Take a photo of the locker bank and its number. Big stations have dozens of banks that look the same, and "it was near a staircase" is hard for staff to work with.

Sizes and fees: what fits

Sizes and prices differ by operator and station, and the fee is shown on the locker itself. As one real example, here is the published list for Tokyu Railways station lockers in Tokyo:

Fees are per use, at the time of writing (October 2026). Tokyu notes that from August 2026 some of its lockers use time-based pricing, so the touch panel shows the amount that applies.

The thing that catches people out is the width. Most lockers are only about 34–35 cm wide inside, so it's the narrowest side of your suitcase that decides whether it goes in. A carry-on-size bag whose narrowest side is under that can fit a large locker; a full-size check-in suitcase normally needs one of the tall extra-large lockers, and a bank may only have a handful of those.

Time limits: don't leave a bag for a week

Station lockers are for a few hours or a couple of days, not long-term storage. Using Tokyu's rules as an example:

  • Use is generally limited to 3 days.
  • If the locker is still in use after 2:00 a.m., another fee is added, so leaving a bag overnight costs more than one fee.
  • Lockers can be used from the first train to the last train.
  • After the limit, your bag is moved to the line's coin locker center. It is kept for 1 month from when you started, and you pay one locker fee for each day it was stored when you collect it.

Other railways have their own rules, printed on the locker door, but the pattern (a short limit, a 2:00 a.m. day change, then removal to storage) is very common. Airports can be more generous: coin lockers at Haneda Airport can be used for up to 7 days, after which items are moved to a storage area and kept for 30 days.

Also don't put in anything heavy or delicate. Tokyu, for example, doesn't allow items over 30 kg, valuables, dangerous goods, animals, food that spoils, or anything smelly.

Finding a free locker (or reserving one)

At popular stations, the big lockers fill up fast at busy times. These tools save a lot of walking:

  • Multi eCube (JR East and other stations): you can check free lockers online and reserve one from 2:00 a.m. on the same date one month before you need it. Reserving needs a Multi eCube membership and a registered credit card, and costs a ¥500 reservation fee per locker on top of the locker fee. A reservation lasts up to 3 days including the start day. You open the locker with your reservation number or the QR code that's emailed to you. Free cancellation is possible until 2:00 a.m. on the start day.
  • JR East app: shows coin locker availability at JR East stations (the app's store page is in Japanese).
  • ICOCA Locker Search (JR West): shows free lockers in real time by size at Osaka, Shin-Osaka, Kyoto, Okayama, Hiroshima and Hakata stations.
  • Smart lockers on JR West's list: some can be reserved by smartphone up to 24 hours ahead.
  • HANDS FREE KYOTO: Kyoto City's official site maps coin lockers, storage counters and luggage delivery points around Kyoto. See also our Kyoto Station guide.

For Tokyo's two biggest stations, our Tokyo Station guide and Shinjuku Station guide cover where the lockers are.

When the lockers are full: staffed storage

If every big locker is taken, or your bag simply doesn't fit, look for a staffed counter. Options listed by JR West and Kyoto City include:

  • Station storage counters. JR West's group offers temporary storage, booked by smartphone, at Kyoto, Shin-Osaka, Osaka, Hiroshima and Hakata stations. Shin-Osaka also has the Shin-Osaka Luggage Station, which takes advance reservations and has multilingual staff.
  • Booking apps such as ecbo cloak and Radical Storage, which let you reserve space at shops and other places near stations. Kyoto City's site lists these for luggage that doesn't fit in lockers.
  • Your hotel. Some hotels hold bags before check-in or after check-out (JR West says guests of its group hotels may be able to do this). Ask your hotel.

Or skip the locker: send the bag ahead

On a moving day, it's often easier not to carry the suitcase at all. In Kyoto, the HANDS FREE KYOTO services can take luggage from Kyoto Station to your accommodation the same day if you complete the procedure before 2 p.m., and between accommodations in the city. JR West's group runs same-day delivery from Kyoto, Shin-Osaka, Osaka, Hiroshima and Hakata stations to hotels, and some lockers in Osaka and Kyoto offer same-day delivery to participating hotels. For nationwide hotel-to-hotel and hotel-to-airport delivery, see our luggage forwarding guide.

Flying home from Kansai Airport? You can book luggage delivery between Osaka/Kyoto hotels and Kansai Airport on Klook (affiliate link). On Klook at the time of writing it was rated 4.9 (1.9K reviews), from US$14.89; prices vary by number of bags and option.

A few things station staff wish visitors knew

  • Check out of the hotel with a plan. If you'll sightsee before an evening train, decide which station's lockers you'll use, and reserve if you have a big suitcase.
  • Use a locker at the station you leave from, not the one you visit. Then you don't have to come back across town for the bag.
  • Big suitcases on the shinkansen. On the Tokaido, Sanyo, Kyushu and Nishi Kyushu shinkansen, bags over 160 cm in total dimensions need a seat reserved with an oversized baggage area. See our shinkansen guide.
  • Large bags on city buses. Kyoto City says bags that are too big or too many may be refused on its buses and subway, which is another good reason to lock up or send the suitcase before sightseeing.
  • Lost your key or receipt? Don't force the door. Contact the locker's management center; Tokyu, for example, lists phone numbers for its coin locker centers for exactly this. And if you left something on a train instead, see what to do.
