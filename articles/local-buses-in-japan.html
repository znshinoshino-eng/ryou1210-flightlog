Local Buses in Japan: How to Get On, Pay and Get Off

Transit guide · Written by someone who works on Japan's railways · Checked November 2026

Short answer: Japan's local buses use two systems. On flat-fare city buses you pay one fixed price — either when you board (Tokyo) or when you get off (Kyoto). On distance-based buses you take a numbered ticket when you board, and pay the fare shown for your number on the screen at the front when you get off. An IC card such as Suica or ICOCA works on most city buses; otherwise have coins or a ¥1,000 note ready, because the fare box cannot handle large bills.

Trains get most of the attention, but in cities like Kyoto the bus is often the most direct way to reach temples, shrines and neighbourhoods away from the rail lines. The system looks confusing the first time — two doors, a ticket machine, a screen full of numbers — but it follows a simple logic once you know which of the two fare systems your bus uses.

The two fare systems at a glance

Almost every local bus in Japan works one of these two ways. Which one applies depends on the city and the route, not on the bus itself, so check the pattern when you board.

Flat-fare buses: Tokyo and Kyoto do it in opposite ways

Tokyo (Toei Bus, 23 wards). Board through the front door and pay as you get on: the flat fare is ¥210 for adults, by IC card or cash. Children pay ¥110 in cash, or ¥105 by IC card. Then walk to the back and get off through the rear door — no payment, no ticket check on the way out. Outside the 23 wards, in the Tama area, Toei buses switch to the distance system described below: rear-door boarding and payment when you get off. If you will ride several times in one day, the Toei Bus One-day Pass (¥500 for adults, ¥250 for children, valid on Toei buses in the 23 wards on the day of purchase) can pay for itself in three rides.

Kyoto (Kyoto City Bus, flat-fare zone). Here it is the other way round. Board through the rear door — no payment yet — and sit or stand anywhere you like. When your stop is called, go to the front, put the flat fare of ¥230 (¥120 for children aged 6–11) in the fare box next to the driver, and get off through the front door. Most sightseeing routes in central Kyoto are inside this flat-fare zone, which makes the bus simple once you trust that you really do pay at the end, not the start. If you will ride several times in one day, the Kyoto City Subway and Bus 1-Day Ticket covers city buses and the subway for a day — from US$3.85 on Klook at the time of writing, rated 4.5 with over 20,000 bookings there: Kyoto City Subway and Bus 1 Day Ticket on Klook (affiliate link).

One Kyoto exception worth knowing: the Sightseeing Limited Express buses (EX100 and EX101), which link Kyoto Station with major sights such as Kiyomizu-dera and Ginkakuji, work like Tokyo buses — board at the front, pay on boarding (¥500 for adults, ¥250 for children), and get off at the rear.

Distance-based buses and the numbered ticket

On routes where the fare depends on how far you ride — Kyoto routes that leave the flat-fare zone, Toei buses in the Tama area, and most suburban and rural buses nationwide — you will use the numbered-ticket system:

1. Board through the rear door and take a numbered ticket (seiriken) from the small machine by the door — one per person. This number marks where you got on. If you forget to take one, your fare is calculated from the route's first stop, so it pays to grab it.

2. Watch the screen at the front of the bus. It lists ticket numbers with the fare you would pay if you got off at the next stop, and it updates at every stop.

3. When your stop is announced, press one of the stop buttons, go to the front, drop your numbered ticket into the fare box, and pay the amount shown for your number. A child's fare is half the adult fare on the screen, rounded up to the nearest ¥10. Then exit through the front door.

The screen only shows numbers, no language skills needed: match your ticket number to the price, and that is what you pay.

Paying: IC cards, coins and the ¥1,000-note rule

IC cards are easiest. The ten nationwide cards — Kitaca, Suica, PASMO, TOICA, manaca, ICOCA, PiTaPa, SUGOCA, nimoca and Hayakaken — are mutually accepted on transport across Japan, including Kyoto City buses and Toei buses. Where you tap depends on the system: on pay-on-boarding buses you tap the reader as you get on; on pay-on-exit buses you tap when you get off. If you are still deciding which card to buy, our guide to IC cards in Japan compares the main options. One caution: a small number of rural bus lines still do not accept IC cards, so carry some coins as a backup.

Cash works too, with one limit. Fare boxes take coins, and there is a slot for a ¥1,000 note if you need change — the machine returns coins. What the fare box cannot do is handle ¥5,000 or ¥10,000 notes, and the driver cannot change them for you. Break large notes at a convenience store before you ride. Payment is by cash or IC card only: on Kyoto City buses, credit and debit cards are not accepted, and the Japan Rail Pass is not valid on city buses either — it covers JR trains and a small number of JR buses, not municipal ones.

Getting off, and a few final tips

Stops are announced and shown on the front display, usually in English as well as Japanese in the big cities. Press a stop button as soon as your stop is announced — the bus will not stop otherwise. On pay-on-exit buses, have your fare or IC card in hand before the doors open so the queue behind you keeps moving; buses keep to tight schedules and drivers will wait, but a ready fare makes the whole thing stress-free.

Buses pair well with trains rather than replacing them. In Kyoto especially, a common pattern is the subway or a JR line for the long cross-city leg, then a bus for the last stretch to a temple or shrine — see our Kyoto and Osaka getting-around guide for how the pieces fit together. And if you are travelling between cities overnight rather than across town, that is a different kind of bus entirely: our night bus guide covers those.

Once you have ridden one flat-fare bus and one numbered-ticket bus, you can ride buses anywhere in Japan. The doors, the screen and the fare box will be in the same places — only the prices change.
