Getting to Haneda's Observation Decks: Trains, IC Cards, and Tickets Explained

Transit guide · Written by someone who actually works with Japan's rail system

Haneda Airport is well connected by train, but if you've never used Japan's railways before, the number of ticket options and card types can be confusing. Here's the short version, without the tourist-site fluff.

Two train lines serve Haneda

There are two separate railways into the airport, and they're not interchangeable:

  • Tokyo Monorail — connects Haneda to Hamamatsucho Station, which sits on the JR Yamanote Line (the loop line that circles central Tokyo) and the JR Keihin-Tōhoku Line. Good if you're heading toward Tokyo Station, Shinjuku, or anywhere on the Yamanote loop.
  • Keikyu Airport Line — connects Haneda to Shinagawa Station, and through-runs directly onto the Toei Asakusa subway line, reaching areas like Asakusa without changing trains. Good if your hotel is near Shinagawa or along the Asakusa Line.

Both lines stop at all three terminals, so which one you take mostly comes down to where you're headed afterward — check a map app for your specific route rather than guessing.

Do you need an IC card?

For getting around Tokyo in general, yes — an IC card (a rechargeable tap-to-ride card) is by far the easiest way to use trains, subways, and buses without buying a paper ticket for every single ride. The two main cards, Suica and PASMO, work identically and are accepted everywhere in the Tokyo area, so it doesn't really matter which one you end up with.

Getting an IC card at Haneda (Terminal 3 arrivals)

If you're arriving as a visitor, there are a few options right in the Terminal 3 arrival area:

  • Welcome Suica — a tourist version of Suica with no deposit. You load it with between ¥1,000 and ¥10,000. It's valid for 28 days from the date of issue (not from first use), and the balance isn't refundable. You can get it from a vending machine near the Monorail entrance, or from the JR East travel service counter just past it — the vending machine is one of the few places at the airport where you can charge an IC card directly with a credit card.
  • PASMO — available from a vending machine near the Keikyu Line entrance. There's a ¥500 card fee on top of whatever balance you load, and unlike Welcome Suica, you generally can't buy or charge it with a credit card at the machine — cash only.
  • Welcome Suica Mobile — if you have a compatible iPhone, you can issue a Welcome Suica digitally through the Wallet app before you even land, skip the line entirely, and it stays valid for 180 days instead of 28.

Lines at the Welcome Suica machine can get long right after international arrivals, so if you're an iPhone user, setting up Welcome Suica Mobile in advance is worth doing.

Can you just tap a regular credit card instead?

Increasingly, yes, on some lines — several private railways and bus operators have added contactless tap-to-ride readers that accept regular contactless credit cards, Apple Pay, and Google Pay directly, no IC card needed. Coverage is improving but still inconsistent between operators and stations, so an IC card remains the more reliable option if you're planning to move around Tokyo a lot. If you want to rely on tap-to-ride, check the specific line's official site in advance to confirm it's supported.
